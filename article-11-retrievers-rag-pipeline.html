Every piece is ready. Article 9 got Tidewave's docs loaded and split. Article 10 got them embedded and searchable in a vector store. What's missing is the piece that turns "searchable" into "answers questions": wrapping that search in LangChain's retriever interface, then wiring retrieval and generation together into one chain. That's this article, and it's the payoff for the entire RAG arc.

The whole pipeline, in one place

Before building anything new, it's worth seeing every piece from Articles 9 and 10 sitting together, since a retriever is built directly on top of them:

python
from langchain_text_splitters import RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter
from langchain_chroma import Chroma
from langchain_openai import OpenAIEmbeddings

docs = load_directory("tidewave_docs")                      # Article 9
chunks = RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter(                     # Article 9
    chunk_size=300, chunk_overlap=50,
).split_documents(docs)

vectorstore = Chroma.from_documents(                         # Article 10
    documents=chunks,
    embedding=OpenAIEmbeddings(model="text-embedding-3-small"),
    collection_name="tidewave_docs",
)

retriever = vectorstore.as_retriever(search_kwargs={"k": 1})

load_directory is the plain-Python loader from Article 9. Everything else is exactly what Article 10 built, now collapsed into the handful of lines it actually takes once you're not learning each piece for the first time, including the named collection Article 10 recommended.

To run everything in this article without an API key, swap the embedding= line for Article 10's normalized ToyEmbeddings(), and add collection_configuration={"hnsw": {"space": "cosine"}} alongside it, for a reason the k=2 note below makes concrete. Every output shown in this article was produced exactly that way.

The retriever interface

That last line is the new piece: a retriever is, unsurprisingly by now, a Runnable. Give it a string, get back a list of Documents.

python
docs = retriever.invoke("How do refunds work?")

Real output, running this against Tidewave's docs with the toy embeddings standing in, k=1 so a single, clearly-best chunk:

output
## Refunds

Tidewave issues refunds for accidental duplicate charges or billing errors
on our end. Refund requests should be sent to billing@tidewave.example
within 30 days of the charge. Refunds are not issued for partial months
of usage after a downgrade.

## Failed payments

That trailing ## Failed payments heading is Article 9's chunk_overlap doing exactly what it did there, carrying the next section's heading into this chunk's tail rather than cutting it off cold.

Worth a direct, honest note on k values above 1 with a toy vectorizer this coarse, because it's Article 10's distance gotcha coming back in a subtler form. At k=2 on Chroma's default Euclidean distance, the second slot doesn't go to the obviously related "Changing your plan" chunk, which mentions both plans and refunds. It goes to a chunk with no real connection to refunds at all, such as "Failed payments". The numbers say why. A chunk containing none of the seven vocabulary words is an all-zero vector, and normalization can't fix it: there's no direction to scale to length 1, so toy_vectorize leaves it at zero. An all-zero vector sits at a Euclidean distance of exactly 1.0 from every normalized query, while "Changing your plan", only partly about refunds (cosine similarity 0.45), sits at about 1.05, further away. So every empty chunk outranks every partly relevant one, and when several empty chunks tie at exactly 1.0, which of them lands in slot 2 isn't something Chroma guarantees. Switch the collection to cosine distance, as recommended above, and "Changing your plan" correctly comes second, with the empty chunks behind it.

A real embedding model doesn't produce all-zero vectors, and its dense, continuous vectors make exact ties rare, so this particular failure is an artifact of the toy vectorizer, not something you'll hit day to day with OpenAIEmbeddings or GoogleGenerativeAIEmbeddings. The broader lesson holds regardless of embedding model, though: never assume the second, third, and fourth results at a given k are anywhere near as relevant as the first, and it's worth actually looking at what lands in your context window rather than trusting k blindly. More on that trade-off further down.

Formatting retrieved documents into context

A prompt needs a string, not a list of Document objects. One small function bridges that:

python
from langchain_core.documents import Document

def format_docs(docs: list[Document]) -> str:
    return "\n\n".join(d.page_content for d in docs)

Nothing clever, join the retrieved chunks with a blank line between them. This is the piece that turns a list of search results into something you can drop straight into a prompt template's {context}.

Assembling the RAG chain

This is where Article 5, 6, and 7 all actually meet:

python
from langchain_core.prompts import ChatPromptTemplate
from langchain_core.runnables import RunnablePassthrough
from langchain_core.output_parsers import StrOutputParser
from models import get_model

prompt = ChatPromptTemplate.from_messages([
    ("system", "Answer the question using only the context below.\n\nContext:\n{context}"),
    ("human", "{question}"),
])
model = get_model()

rag_chain = (
    {"context": retriever | format_docs, "question": RunnablePassthrough()}
    | prompt
    | model
    | StrOutputParser()
)

get_model() is Article 5's project helper, reused exactly as intended, the assistant's actual answering step is provider-agnostic by construction, same as every other piece of Tidewave's code in this series.

Fig. 1 — The same question fans out to two branches (retrieve+format, and pass through untouched), merges into a dict, then flows through the ordinary sequential chain from Article 7.

Read the dict at the top the way Article 7 taught: a plain dict piped into the next step gets auto-coerced into a RunnableParallel, running both entries against the same input. "context": retriever | format_docs is itself a two-step chain, retrieve, then join into a string, one of the two parallel branches. "question": RunnablePassthrough() is the other branch, carrying the original question through completely unchanged, exactly Article 7's pattern for keeping raw input alongside a transformed version. Both branches run against whatever string you call rag_chain.invoke() with; their outputs land in a dict with keys context and question, which is exactly the shape prompt, built with those same two placeholders, expects. From there it's the ordinary chain from Article 7: prompt into model into a plain string.

Running the whole thing for real

The retrieval and formatting steps need no API key, so they're worth running exactly as written. The generation step does need one, so here's the same honest stand-in Article 8 used, a RunnableLambda in place of a real model, purely so the full chain can execute end to end with genuine output:

python
from langchain_core.messages import AIMessage
from langchain_core.runnables import RunnableLambda

def fake_model(prompt_value) -> AIMessage:
    messages = prompt_value.to_messages()
    context_length = len(messages[0].content)
    return AIMessage(content=f"[would answer using {context_length} chars of context]")

rag_chain = (
    {"context": retriever | format_docs, "question": RunnablePassthrough()}
    | prompt
    | RunnableLambda(fake_model)
    | StrOutputParser()
)

result = rag_chain.invoke("How do refunds work?")
print(result)

Genuine output:

output
[would answer using 337 chars of context]
Fig. 2 — The actual journey one question takes through the chain, with the real numbers this article's code produces.

That 337 is real, it's the actual formatted system message, retrieved context and all, that made it all the way through retrieval, formatting, and prompt construction before reaching the (fake) model. Swap RunnableLambda(fake_model) back for get_model() and nothing else in rag_chain changes; that's the entire point of everything being built on the same Runnable interface since Article 5.

The k trade-off

k is a real dial, not a value to set once and forget. Too small, and a genuinely relevant chunk sitting just outside the cutoff never reaches the model at all, the assistant answers confidently from an incomplete picture. Too large, and low-relevance chunks, the kind that showed up unpredictably in the k=2 test above, start diluting the context, sometimes measurably hurting answer quality even though the correct chunk is technically still in there somewhere. There's no universal right number; two to five is a reasonable starting range for short documentation chunks like Tidewave's, tuned against how your own retrieval actually performs. And k is always a count, never a quality bar: a retriever returns its k nearest chunks even when none of them is actually relevant, which is why the prompt above tells the model to answer "using only the context below" rather than trusting that relevant context arrived.

One more retrieval option worth knowing exists without a full detour: vectorstore.as_retriever(search_type="mmr") switches from plain similarity search to Maximal Marginal Relevance, which actively favors a more diverse set of chunks over the single most-similar cluster, useful when a query might be answered by several different sections of a document rather than one concentrated passage. "similarity", plain nearest-neighbor search, is the default and the right starting point for most cases, including everything in this article.

What's next

Tidewave's assistant can now genuinely answer questions from its own documentation, not just what the model already knew. It does so with a fixed pipeline, though: every question triggers a retrieval, whether it needs one or not. LangChain's own current RAG tutorial goes a step further, to agentic RAG, where retrieval is a tool the model decides when to call. The chain built here is the mechanism underneath that, and this series gets to the agentic version in Article 15, once tools (Article 12) and agents (Article 13) are in place: there, retrieval itself becomes one of the agent's tools. What it still can't do is take an action, actually check a specific customer's subscription status rather than describe how billing generally works. Next up: tools, the piece that lets the assistant do something in the world instead of only describing it.
